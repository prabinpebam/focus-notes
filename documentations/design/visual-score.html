Design

Visual vs verbose score

A 0–100 score that tells whether a surface shows its information or describes it, and the minimum a surface needs before it's done.

TL;DR

Every surface that presents a task or a state gets a visual vs verbose score (VVS) from five criteria worth 20 points each: a visual anchor, glanceable in 3 seconds, the right element for each piece of information, text economy against a word budget, and clear hierarchy. A surface is acceptable at VVS ≥ 70 with no criterion below 10; first-run steps and empty states need ≥ 80. The journeys measure the anchor and the words; the design-system-reviewer scores the rest from light and dark screenshots, and the scores go into the gate evidence. Prefer the right element over a sentence: a profile card, not "connected as @login". (D-127)

Why

The words belong to the writer

Focus Notes is a calm, Markdown-native writing app. The note is the text; everything around it should be seen, not read (CP-CALM-01).

First impressions

The first run and empty states decide whether the app feels calm. They carry the highest bar.

Anyone can follow it

Setup is for people who've never heard of GitHub (D-124). A face and a button say "this is you" faster than a sentence.

The visual quality checklist checks geometry; Words checks phrasing. The VVS checks the step between them: whether the information is shown with the right element at all.

The five criteria

Each criterion scores 0–20. The anchors below fix what 0, 10, and 20 look like; scores in between are allowed.

Criterion0 looks like10 looks like20 looks like
1. Visual anchor
A dominant non-text element tells the purpose at a glance.		 Words and buttons only. A non-text element 24–47 px, or a large one that's decorative rather than about the purpose. One non-text element of 48 px or more, in view, that says what the surface is for: an avatar, app icon, illustration, preview, or status graphic.
2. Glanceable
The purpose is clear within 3 seconds.		 No title, or a generic one ("Setup"); the purpose is found only by reading several sentences. The title names the purpose but runs 7–10 words, or the key fact sits in a second or third sentence. A title of 6 words or fewer and at most one body sentence of 20 words or fewer on the primary path.
3. Right element
Each piece of information uses the element made for it (mapping).		 The surface's main information is prose describing state the UI could show. Some information uses the right element; at least one piece is still a sentence or a generic list. Every piece uses its element. No sentence restates what an element already shows.
4. Text economy
Interface words stay within the surface's budget.		 Twice the budget or more. 1.5 times the budget, or any paragraph over 2 lines. Within the budget, no paragraph over 2 lines, details behind disclosure (More options, Having trouble?).
5. Hierarchy
One focal point and a dominant primary action.		 Everything the same size and weight, or two primary-looking actions. A focal point that competes with another (title and anchor at similar weight), or ramp steps used inconsistently. One focal point, distinct steps of the interface type ramp (title, emphasis, body, secondary or caption), and exactly one primary action that stands out.

How it's measured

  1. Journeys measure criteria 1 and 4

    In the visual-quality journeys, at the default window size, light and dark, on each surface's primary path.

  2. The reviewer scores criteria 2, 3, and 5

    The design-system-reviewer scores them from light and dark screenshots against the anchors above, and may lower criterion 1 with a reason.

  3. The evidence records all five

    One row per surface in the work package's evidence record and the gate evidence.

CriterionMeasured byRule
1. Visual anchorJourney, reviewer may lowerThe largest non-text element fully in view (avatar, icon, illustration, preview, progress graphic), by its rendered box's shorter side: 48 px or more scores 20, 24–47 px scores 10, otherwise 0. The reviewer lowers it, with a reason, when the anchor doesn't express the purpose.
2. GlanceableReviewerTitle words, body sentences, and a 3-second read of the light screenshot.
3. Right elementReviewerEach piece of information checked against the mapping.
4. Text economyJourneyWords W against budget B: 20 × (2B − W) / B, clamped to 0–20 and rounded, so 20 within the budget and 0 at twice it. Capped at 10 if any paragraph wraps to more than 2 lines.
5. HierarchyReviewerThe light and the dark screenshot; when they differ, the lower score counts.
What counts as a word
  • Counted: visible interface text on the primary path, the state a person sees first without opening anything: titles, body, labels, buttons, links, and step labels. Numbers count as words.
  • Not counted: text under a closed disclosure, tooltips, text only for assistive technology, the title bar, and the person's own content and data (note text, file and note names, search results, a name and @login from GitHub).

Where it applies

Scored

  • Every new or changed surface that presents a task or a state, at its work package's definition of done
  • Existing surfaces, at the next stage gate; anything below the threshold goes into the feedback queue

Exempt

  • Not scored: menus, tooltips, toasts, toolbars, and the trigger. Their job is a command or a word; the checklist and Words cover them.
  • Criterion 4: surfaces whose purpose is text: the note canvas, help pages, and legal notices. Their anchor may be the content's own display heading.
  • Criterion 1: confirmations, short alerts whose question is their anchor.

With a criterion exempt, the VVS is the other four scaled to 100 (their sum × 1.25, rounded), and the thresholds stay the same.
